Path 01 · Design philosophy

Design is solving a problem with style.

Understand the operator’s problem first, then make the solution fun to use. These are the three habits I design by, and the techniques I reach for. Each one is shown with a live example below, so you can try it rather than take my word for it.

01

Observe

Before I draw anything, I sit at the operator’s desk during a live show. People rarely describe their real problems in a meeting; they work around them so often that the workaround feels normal. Watching is how those problems surface.

Here is a routing panel of the kind I often walk into. A routing panel is the set of buttons an operator uses to send a video source (a camera, a replay machine) to a destination (a monitor, a recorder). Hover or tap the red marks to see what watching an operator revealed.

  1. 1Cryptic labels. “SRC 07” means nothing, so operators memorise button positions. A new hire can’t touch the panel.
  2. 2No sense of what’s live. Every button looks the same, so nobody can see at a glance what is on air.
  3. 3The commit button is far away. “TAKE” sits in the corner, so every single cut means a reach across the panel.

02

Reduce

Simplify until every element earns its space. In live broadcast, speed is king: an operator has a fraction of a second to make a cut, so I break complex workflows down into the fewest, clearest actions.

03

Improve

Delivery is just the beginning. I support what I design once it’s on air, and relentlessly improve it with what operators tell me. Small changes, shipped often, each one answering something I heard.

Step through the versions and try the panel yourself: pick a source, then press TAKE to put it on air.

In practice

Techniques I reach for

  • Workflow mapping

    I map every step an operator takes, in order, before drawing a single screen. The map shows which steps can merge and which can disappear.

  • Big targets for live hands

    Live operators work fast, in dim rooms, under pressure. The bigger and closer a button is, the faster and more accurately it’s hit, so the controls used most are the largest and nearest.

  • Colour means state

    Colour is reserved for meaning, never decoration. Red is on air, green is up next, and that holds on every panel I build, so nobody has to think about it.

  • Modular layouts

    Every operator watches something different. Pinnable modules let each person assemble their own dashboard from the same reliable building blocks.

  • Guide, don’t train

    The interface should point the way. Plain words, the next step highlighted and a hand offered where people used to get stuck, so it works without a manual.

  • Prototype with operators

    I build working panels early and put them in front of the people who will use them on air. Their hands find the problems faster than any review.
